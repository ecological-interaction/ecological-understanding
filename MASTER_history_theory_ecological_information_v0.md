# History and Theory of Ecological Information
## Baseline Master Document — Bioregional Course


---

## A — Original numbered sequence

For the bioregional course, I think **“History and Theory of Ecological Information”** could become an important conceptual strand. It would explain not merely *how we monitor nature*, but **how humans have decided what constitutes ecological information, how that information became measurable and computable, and how it has subsequently been used to make decisions**.

A useful historical arc would be:

**Observation → Measurement → Classification → Mapping → Sensing → Computation → Prediction → Ecological intelligence**

This gives us a theoretical counterpart to the technical course material.

### 1. Nature as signs — observation before instrumentation

The history should begin well before modern ecology. Ecological information initially exists as **interpreted signs**: flowering, migration, tracks, weather, soil appearance, animal behaviour, seasonal cycles and relationships between organisms.

This introduces an important theoretical distinction:

> **Nature produces signals; people construct information from those signals.**

A bird call, spectral reflection from a leaf, fungal DNA fragment and change in soil moisture are physical phenomena. They become *ecological information* when they are detected, categorized and related to some question.

This provides an excellent foundation for your interest in learning from organisms' senses and communication systems.

### 2. Classification — making nature legible

Natural history gradually formalised observation through specimens, taxonomies, collections and standardized descriptions.

Linnaean taxonomy is important, but the course could move beyond a simple history of taxonomy toward **classification as an information technology**. Naming organisms makes observations comparable across places and observers.

Herbaria, natural-history collections, field notebooks and species catalogues can consequently be treated as early ecological databases.

This raises a recurring course question:

**What is gained—and what disappears—when continuous ecological variation is converted into categories?**

### 3. Ecology — from objects to relationships

The emergence of ecology changes the informational object.

Instead of asking primarily:

**“What organism is this?”**

ecology increasingly asks:

**“What is this organism doing here, and what is it connected to?”**

Haeckel's *Ökologie*, Darwinian relationships, biogeography, succession, food webs, population ecology and ecosystem theory progressively turn nature into **networks, flows, populations and systems**.

The informational unit moves from **specimen → relationship → system**.

### 4. Quantification — ecology becomes measurable

During the twentieth century, quadrats, transects, population sampling, statistics, energy budgets, nutrient cycles and ecosystem modelling make ecological processes increasingly quantitative.

This is where concepts such as **indicator species, biodiversity indices, biomass, productivity, abundance, richness and diversity** become important.

A critical distinction emerges between:

**ecological phenomena → measurements → indicators**

An indicator is not nature itself. It is a deliberately selected compression of ecological complexity.

That observation connects very strongly to your PhD interest in ecological indicators informing technology choice.

### 5. Cybernetics and systems ecology — nature as information

The mid-twentieth century introduces another major conceptual transition. Cybernetics, information theory, systems theory and systems ecology introduce ideas of **feedback, communication, regulation, networks and information flows**.

Norbert Wiener, Claude Shannon and ecologists such as Eugene Odum become relevant here.

The ecosystem can increasingly be represented as something resembling a dynamic information-processing system.

But the course should distinguish carefully between **information about ecosystems** and claims that **ecosystems themselves process information**. The latter opens a much richer theoretical discussion involving biosemiotics, cognition and biological signalling.

### 6. Remote sensing — ecology becomes an image of the Earth

Aerial photography and eventually satellite remote sensing produce another transformation:

**observation becomes spatially continuous.**

Instead of measuring selected organisms or plots, scientists can repeatedly observe landscapes, regions and eventually the entire planet.

Spectral information introduces a particularly useful conceptual example for the course. Vegetation can be represented as RGB values, spectral bands and eventually indices such as NDVI.

This fits directly with your **Four Ways of Seeing Green** exercises.

A plant becomes simultaneously:

**an organism → a photograph → spectral measurements → an index → a mapped pixel → a time series.**

Students can therefore experience the history of ecological information through computation rather than only reading about it.

### 7. Molecular ecology — organisms become information

Genetics introduces an almost opposite movement in scale.

Remote sensing expands outward toward planetary observation while molecular biology moves inward toward DNA.

DNA barcoding, metabarcoding, eDNA, metagenomics and increasingly environmental RNA allow environments to be read through molecular traces.

A litre of water or gram of soil can consequently become an enormous ecological information source.

This produces the **macro ↔ micro** structure already central to the Ecological Interactions seminar:

**Satellite → landscape → drone → organism → sensor → microscope → DNA**

### 8. The networked environment

IoT sensors, autonomous monitoring stations, bioacoustics, camera traps, drones, satellites and molecular sampling increasingly operate together.

Ecological observation therefore moves from individual instruments toward **ecological information infrastructures**.

This is especially important for the distributed bioregional course because the object of study is no longer simply a sensor. It is the complete chain:

**Phenomenon → signal → sensor → data → transmission → storage → processing → indicator → visualization → interpretation → decision → intervention**

That chain could become one of the central diagrams of the whole course.

### 9. From monitoring to prediction

Machine learning introduces another epistemological change.

Earlier ecological information systems primarily answered:

**What is there?**  
**What happened?**

Contemporary systems increasingly ask:

**What is happening now?**  
**What will happen next?**  
**What should we do about it?**

This moves the history directly into **decision-support systems, ecological digital twins, automated classification, forecasting and AI-assisted ecological reasoning**.

It also creates an important theoretical problem: ecological information becomes increasingly mediated by models that no individual observer can directly perceive.

### 10. Toward ecological intelligence

I would make the final section speculative rather than simply calling it “AI.”

The trajectory becomes:

**Natural history** — observing nature  
→ **Ecology** — understanding relationships  
→ **Environmental monitoring** — measuring change  
→ **Earth observation** — seeing systems across scales  
→ **Ecological computing** — processing environmental signals  
→ **Decision intelligence** — connecting indicators to interventions  
→ **Distributed ecological intelligence** — places learning from one another.

That final step connects particularly well to the architecture we have been developing for Japan, Scotland, African savannah and Terres de l'Ebre nodes. Each place generates a **regional ecological signature**, while shared computational methods allow comparison and learning between bioregions.

This also suggests a strong theoretical proposition for the course:

> **Ecological information is not simply data collected from nature. It is the evolving system through which signals from living environments are selected, measured, classified, transmitted, modelled and translated into human decisions.**

And that gives us three interlocking strands for the wider course: **History and Theory of Ecological Information** explains *how nature became knowable*; **Ecological Monitoring and Computing** teaches *how we make it measurable*; and **Bioregional / Design Practice** asks *what we should do with that knowledge*.

That structure also creates a very clean bridge between the original **Surveilling Nature** course, the **Ecological Interactions** seminar, and *Making Place Computable*.

## B — Nine overlapping intellectual lineages

Yes. The literature is much broader than “ecological information” as a named field. What you are constructing sits at the intersection of **ecology, cybernetics, information theory, perception, semiotics, Earth-system science, environmental sensing, genomics, STS, environmental humanities and computation**. Searching across those traditions produces a much more interesting intellectual genealogy.

I would currently organise the field into **nine overlapping lineages**.

### 1. Organisms perceive informational worlds

Jakob von Uexküll is an unusually important starting point. His concept of **Umwelt** describes the species-specific perceptual world available to an organism. Different organisms therefore inhabit different informational versions of the same physical environment. Contemporary biosemiotics continues to develop this idea, including recent work applying Umwelt analysis directly to multispecies places. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="1"}

**Key thinkers:** Uexküll; Charles Sanders Peirce; Thomas Sebeok; Jesper Hoffmeyer; Kalevi Kull; Timo Maran.

**Key concepts:** Umwelt, signs, semiosis, biosemiotics, ecosemiotics, organism–environment coupling.

Maran and Kull explicitly define **ecosemiotics** around environmental perception, conceptual categorisation and transformation of environments. Significantly, their genealogy explicitly includes Bateson. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="2"}

This could become one of the deepest theoretical foundations for your question:

**What information exists for an organism that does not exist for us?**

---

### 2. Perception as ecological information

A closely related but independently developed tradition comes from James J. Gibson.

Gibson's *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception* (1979) treats perception not simply as internal processing of sensory inputs but as an organism actively picking up information from its environment. His ideas of **affordances, invariants and information pickup** are particularly relevant. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="4"}

There is an intriguing bridge here:

**Uexküll — Umwelt**  
↓  
organism-specific perceptual worlds

**Gibson — affordances**  
↓  
environmental possibilities perceived through action

**Ecological monitoring**  
↓  
technologically extended perception

**Your work**  
↓  
What new ecological affordances become perceptible when instruments extend human sensing?

That is potentially a substantial theoretical argument rather than merely a teaching device.

---

### 3. Information, feedback and the ecology of mind

Here Gregory Bateson becomes pivotal.

His *Steps to an Ecology of Mind* (1972) brought communication theory, cybernetics, anthropology, evolution and ecology together. Bateson's importance is not simply the famous formulation of information as a difference that makes a difference; it is his attempt to understand **pattern, relationship, feedback and mind across organism/environment systems**. Contemporary ecosemiotics explicitly acknowledges this lineage. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="6"}

Around Bateson sits a major constellation:

**Norbert Wiener** — cybernetics and feedback  
**Claude Shannon** — mathematical information theory  
**W. Ross Ashby** — regulation and requisite variety  
**Heinz von Foerster** — second-order cybernetics  
**Humberto Maturana & Francisco Varela** — autopoiesis and cognition  
**Stafford Beer** — viable systems  
**Bateson** — ecology of mind

Shannon's information theory subsequently entered ecology; Robert MacArthur's 1955 work is often identified as an early application of information theory to ecological systems. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="7"}

This gives us a historical transformation:

**information transmitted through communication systems**

becomes

**information circulating through ecological systems**.

---

### 4. Ecosystems as information/energy systems

Howard T. Odum deserves a major place alongside Bateson.

Odum developed systems ecology through energy flows, feedback, networks and ecological modelling and later explicitly considered the role and value of **information** within natural and social systems. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="9"}

His diagrams are particularly interesting for your course because they are effectively an early **visual programming language for ecological systems**.

Odum describes ecosystems as processing **energy, materials and information**. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="10"}

This lineage includes:

**Eugene Odum** — ecosystem ecology  
**Howard T. Odum** — systems ecology  
**Alfred Lotka** — energetics and population systems  
**Raymond Lindeman** — trophic dynamics  
**Robert Ulanowicz** — ecological networks/information theory  
**Ilya Prigogine** — dissipative structures  
**Stuart Kauffman** — self-organisation and complexity.

Kauffman's *The Origins of Order* is especially relevant because it asks how complex biological systems can spontaneously develop organisation and how self-organisation interacts with selection. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="11"}

---

### 5. Planetary information and Gaia

Another branch scales this reasoning to Earth.

James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis proposed that biota and physical environment are dynamically coupled. Their 1974 Gaia paper described atmospheric homeostasis involving the biosphere. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="14"}

The intellectual lineage extends backwards and forwards:

**Alexander von Humboldt**  
→ interconnected nature

**Vladimir Vernadsky**  
→ biosphere

**Alfred Lotka**  
→ organism/environment energetics

**Lovelock + Margulis**  
→ Gaia

**Earth System Science**  
→ coupled planetary processes

**Timothy Lenton**  
→ Earth-system feedback/tipping dynamics

**planetary sensing**  
→ satellites + sensor networks + computation.

This creates another powerful question for the course:

> At what point did Earth cease to be merely *mapped* and become continuously *sensed*?

---

### 6. Information becomes molecular

This is where your suggestion of George Church becomes useful.

Church represents something different from Bateson or Morton. He is less a philosopher of ecological information than an important figure in the technological transition from **reading biological information to writing it**.

His work spans genome sequencing, molecular barcoding, DNA/RNA measurement, genome synthesis and synthetic biology; his Harvard research description explicitly includes measurement, synthesis and modelling of biomedical **and ecological systems**. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="16"}

The genealogy here becomes:

**Darwin** — evolutionary information without genes  
→ **Mendel** — inherited traits  
→ **Watson/Crick/Franklin** — molecular structure  
→ **information metaphor of DNA**  
→ **genomics**  
→ **DNA barcoding**  
→ **metabarcoding / eDNA / eRNA**  
→ **Church / synthetic biology**  
→ **reading AND writing biological systems**.

But this strand needs critics and alternatives too.

Denis Noble argues against treating genes as the uniquely privileged causal level. His systems-biology account stresses feedback across multiple organisational levels. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="18"}

That debate is exceptionally useful educationally:

**Is an organism encoded in its genome—or does biological information emerge through interactions across scales?**

---

### 7. Nonhuman intelligence, meaning and multispecies worlds

Here we move into a particularly fertile contemporary literature.

Eduardo Kohn's *How Forests Think* asks anthropology to consider forms of representation and meaning extending beyond humans. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="20"}

Donna Haraway develops multispecies relations, sympoiesis and “making-with” in *Staying with the Trouble*. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="22"}

Vinciane Despret asks how scientific questions and experimental arrangements affect what animals are capable of revealing to observers. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="24"}

Add:

**Anna Tsing** — multispecies assemblages  
**Eben Kirksey** — multispecies ethnography/emergent ecologies  
**Emanuele Coccia** — plant philosophy  
**Monica Gagliano** — plant signalling/learning research  
**Peter Godfrey-Smith** — animal minds, especially cephalopods  
**Frans de Waal** — animal cognition  
**Eva Meijer** — animal communication/politics  
**James Bridle** — technological/nonhuman intelligence.

Multispecies studies now explicitly describes itself as studying epistemological and ethical consequences of knowing and living with fungi, microorganisms, animals, plants and other forms of life. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="25"}

This directly supports the **“senses of other organisms”** component of Ecological Interactions.

---

### 8. Technology doesn't just observe environments—it constructs them as data

This may be the most directly relevant contemporary literature to **Making Place Computable**.

Jennifer Gabrys should definitely enter your central reading list.

Her *Program Earth: Environmental Sensing Technology and the Making of a Computational Planet* examines distributed environmental sensing and makes the crucial argument that sensors don't simply passively record environments. Sensor technologies participate in producing environments as computational objects. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="27"}

She traces a history from Sputnik through electronic environmental monitoring toward sensorised environments and argues that computational technologies have become environmental while environments themselves increasingly become computational. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="28"}

This is remarkably close to your PhD framing.

I would therefore put:

**Bateson → Gabrys**

as one of the principal axes of the research.

Around Gabrys:

**N. Katherine Hayles** — information, embodiment and computation  
**Jussi Parikka** — media archaeology/geology of media  
**Shannon Mattern** — infrastructures and urban information  
**Paul Edwards** — climate knowledge infrastructures  
**Lisa Parks** — satellites and media infrastructure  
**Benjamin Bratton** — planetary computation  
**Yuk Hui** — digital objects, technodiversity/cosmotechnics  
**Gilbert Simondon** — technology, information and milieu.

Simondon looks particularly important. *Individuation in Light of Notions of Form and Information* directly connects information, organisms, milieu, thermodynamics, cybernetics and technical objects. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="29"}

Yuk Hui then carries parts of this tradition into digital systems, examining digital objects relationally within technical systems and milieux. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="30"}

### 9. Ecology as a philosophical problem

This is where Timothy Morton belongs.

Morton's importance is quite different from Church's. Morton questions the conceptual categories through which ecological phenomena become thinkable.

The sequence:

*Ecology Without Nature*  
→ *The Ecological Thought*  
→ *Hyperobjects*  
→ *Dark Ecology*  
→ *Being Ecological*

challenges conventional ideas of “nature,” environmental separation and scale. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="32"}

**Hyperobjects** is particularly relevant to ecological information because phenomena such as climate change exceed ordinary human spatial and temporal perception. They become knowable through **distributed measurements, models, visualisations and computational infrastructures**. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="33"}

That produces a striking connection:

**Morton describes the epistemological problem.**

**Remote sensing and ecological computing provide mechanisms through which parts of the otherwise imperceptible phenomenon become detectable.**

And Bruno Latour belongs very close to this discussion. His later work on Gaia and **Critical Zones** asks how scientific instruments, representations, organisms, soils, atmospheres and political institutions participate in making terrestrial conditions knowable. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="35"}

## C — Provisional intellectual map and accompanying discussion

## A provisional intellectual map

I think we can now construct something considerably more interesting than a chronological bibliography:

**PERCEPTION**  
Uexküll → Gibson → affordances → animal sensing

↓  

**SIGNS & MEANING**  
Peirce → Bateson → Sebeok → Hoffmeyer → Kull → biosemiotics

↓  

**FEEDBACK & SYSTEMS**  
Wiener → Shannon → Bateson → Odum → Maturana/Varela → systems ecology

↓  

**PLANET**  
Humboldt → Vernadsky → Lovelock/Margulis → Earth System Science → Lenton

↓  

**BIOLOGICAL INFORMATION**  
Darwin → genetics → molecular biology → genomics → Church → eDNA/eRNA → synthetic biology

↓  

**COMPLEXITY**  
Prigogine → Kauffman → Noble → network/ecosystem modelling

↓  

**MULTISPECIES KNOWLEDGE**  
Haraway → Despret → Tsing → Kohn → multispecies studies

↓  

**ENVIRONMENTAL COMPUTATION**  
Simondon → Hayles → Gabrys → Hui → Bratton → planetary computation

↓  

**ECOLOGICAL PHILOSOPHY**  
Deleuze/Guattari → Latour → Stengers → Morton → contemporary environmental humanities

↓  

**MAKING PLACE COMPUTABLE**  
remote sensing + molecular sensing + sensor networks + ecological indicators + models + AI + regional comparison.

The important discovery here is that **“ecological information” could operate as the connective concept between traditions that are normally taught separately**.

There is also a very useful tension running through the whole literature:

> **Does ecological information already exist in the world waiting to be detected, or is information produced through the relationship between organism, instrument, observer, model and environment?**

Uexküll, Gibson, Bateson, Shannon, Odum, Church, Haraway, Latour, Morton and Gabrys would give radically different answers to that question.

That is potentially the **central theoretical question of the History and Theory strand**.

I would next expand this into a proper **academic literature map of perhaps 50–80 thinkers**, arranged by these intellectual lineages, with for each person **dates → discipline → key concept → 1–3 essential publications → relationship to ecological information → relevance to your course/PhD**. That would give us the scholarly foundation from which we can then reduce it to a teachable reading list.
